## Engineering summary
Mahāvīrācārya was a ninth-century Jain mathematician whose *Gaṇita-sāra-saṅgraha* organized arithmetic, mensuration, commercial calculation, series, combinations, and algebraic procedures into teachable rules and worked problems. The engineering value of the treatise is not a machine or instrument; it is a computational handbook for reliable calculation in surveying, trade, architecture, calendrical practice, and mathematical education.

## Historical summary
Mahāvīrācārya, often dated approximately 800–870 CE, worked in the mathematical culture of early medieval India under the Rashtrakuta milieu. His treatise is described by MacTutor and the MAA as among the earliest surviving Indian works devoted entirely to mathematics rather than embedding mathematics inside astronomy. The work extended and clarified topics associated with Aryabhata and Brahmagupta while presenting them as reusable procedures.

## Modern relevance
The treatise models a durable engineering pattern: convert mathematical knowledge into named operations, examples, edge cases, and problem classes. That pattern survives in programming textbooks, numerical-methods manuals, spreadsheet functions, engineering handbooks, and algorithm libraries.

## Who they were
Mahāvīrācārya was a Jain mathematician, not to be confused with Mahavira the Jain tīrthaṅkara. The title “ācārya” marks him as a teacher. His reputation rests on *Gaṇita-sāra-saṅgraha*, a Sanskrit compendium that gathered practical and theoretical mathematics into a form suitable for instruction.

## Historical context
Indian mathematical astronomy had already produced sophisticated computation, but mathematical rules were often bound to astronomical settings. Mahāvīrācārya's importance lies in making mathematics stand as an independent discipline. That separation mattered because merchants, builders, administrators, and teachers needed calculation methods even when they were not computing planetary positions.

## Major contributions
- Systematic treatment of terminology, arithmetic operations, fractions, rule-of-three reasoning, mixtures, series, geometry, excavations, shadows, and combinatorial calculations.
- Pedagogical conversion of abstract rules into worked examples.
- Clear continuation of Indian algebraic practice, including methods for unknown quantities and root extraction.
- Preservation of a computational culture in which mathematics was evaluated by whether a trained practitioner could reproduce a correct result.
